Joanna Fu on Chaos Body Shop

On the artist’s book in “Subtropical Signals” at DECK

Author
傅爾得 Joanna Fu
Translation
HSU, Che-jui 許哲睿
Exhibition
Subtropical Signals: Contemporary Image-Making from Taiwan · DECK

This English translation is by HSU, Che-jui. In the event of any discrepancy, the Chinese original is authoritative. Chinese original 中文原文

On a table at the centre of the gallery sits an artist’s book by HSU, Che-jui, a young artist born in 2003. At first glance, it seems markedly different from the works on the walls. The artists discussed earlier begin with rivers, local communities and lived experience, whereas HSU belongs to a generation that has grown up amid social media, digital platforms and artificial intelligence.

Yet I believe his work is closely connected to the exhibition’s central proposition. “Subtropical Signals” began with a simple observation: in Taiwan, the world’s most advanced semiconductor factories and religious processions with centuries of history exist on the same land.

In “Chaos Body Shop,” HSU gives this condition a tangible form. Starting with his own ID photograph, he generates more than four hundred AI portraits of different identities. Religious deities, folk legends and tarot archetypes, alongside anime characters, popular culture figures and political leaders, are placed in a single sequence, bringing together images that originally belonged to distinct cultural contexts. At the same time, the artist takes apart, transcribes and rearranges the “Diamond Sutra” according to the Zhuyin phonetic symbols, detaching its once meaning-bearing text from its established context and returning it to sound and form themselves.

The work is concerned not with AI technology itself, but with how identities, idols and authority are produced. Deities, political figures, anime characters and internet culture are viewed, circulated and consumed within the same system, gradually blurring boundaries that once seemed stable. As images become infinitely reproducible and information is endlessly shared, belief, identification and truth also emerge as the outcomes of an ongoing process of construction.

In a sense, this artist’s book also forms a bridge to the exhibition as a whole. If the other works explore how history, belief, family, migration and local cultures shape Taiwan, HSU confronts a distinctly contemporary condition: signals left by different eras no longer simply coexist. In the digital world, they are continually spliced together, translated and reproduced, shaping how people understand truth, authority and belief.
